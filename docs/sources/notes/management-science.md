# Management-science and business-architecture definitions of (business / organizational) capability

Scope: a source register for the BCO's draft commitments. BCO commitments are abbreviated in the register as:

- **C1 Bearer**: a Business Capability is a disposition borne by an Organization (not a person, system or role).
- **C2 Basis**: its material basis is the organization's member agents in their arrangement; artifacts (technology, assets) are enabling conditions, not basis.
- **C3 Realization**: it is realized in business processes (the capability is not itself the process/routine).
- **C4 Ability vs assignment**: having the ability is distinct from being assigned / authorized / responsible.
- **C5 Description layer**: maps, assessments, maturity ratings, sourcing statements and target states are information content entities *about* capabilities.
- **C6 Three questions**: admissibility / possession / adequacy are kept apart.

Verification status: entries marked **[verified]** had their bibliographic data and/or definition confirmed from a page fetched or a search result in this session. Entries marked **[from prior knowledge — verify]** give the standard citation and definition as I know them, but I could not open the full text in this session (paywall, scanned PDF, or Cloudflare block); the report writer should treat page numbers and wording in those entries as needing a check against the publisher PDF before they are put in the spec. Citation counts were not reliably verifiable and are omitted except where noted.

Authority scale used: **Seminal peer-reviewed** (field-defining, heavily cited journal article) > **Peer-reviewed** (journal or refereed conference) > **Practitioner book / report** > **Vendor / analyst**.

## Resource-based view (Wernerfelt 1984; Barney 1991; Grant 1991; Amit & Schoemaker 1993)

### Takeaway
The RBV founders give the BCO its *bearer* (the firm) and its *ability/potential* reading, but they disagree on whether a capability is a kind of resource (Barney) or something distinct from resources that deploys them (Grant; Amit & Schoemaker). Grant and Amit & Schoemaker are the strongest RBV anchors for the BCO: both make capability a firm-level capacity whose exercise is organizational processes/routines, which matches C1 and C3; none of them addresses C4–C6.

### Cited Findings

**R1. Wernerfelt, B. (1984). "A resource-based view of the firm." *Strategic Management Journal* 5(2): 171–180. DOI 10.1002/smj.4250050207.** [bibliographic data from prior knowledge — verify; the article is cited as RBV origin in Offerman et al. 2017, ref. [12]] — [Offerman et al. 2017](https://liacs.leidenuniv.nl/~plaata1/Offerman2017_SLR.pdf)
- Licence/access: Wiley, paywalled.
- Definition: defines *resources* (tangible and intangible assets tied semi-permanently to the firm), not capability as a separate category.
- C1 silent-to-agrees (the firm is the holder of resources); C2 silent; C3 silent; C4–C6 silent. Value to BCO: historical origin only; Offerman et al. identify the RBV as one of the two literature streams from which "business capability" descends (the other being enterprise architecture) — [Offerman et al. 2017, p. 384](https://liacs.leidenuniv.nl/~plaata1/Offerman2017_SLR.pdf).
- Authority: seminal peer-reviewed.

**R2. Barney, J. (1991). "Firm resources and sustained competitive advantage." *Journal of Management* 17(1): 99–120. DOI 10.1177/014920639101700108.** [from prior knowledge — verify]
- Licence/access: SAGE, paywalled.
- Definition (paraphrase): firm resources are a broad category that *includes* capabilities alongside assets, organizational processes, attributes, information and knowledge the firm controls (definition on p. 101, to verify); sustained advantage requires resources that are valuable, rare, imperfectly imitable and non-substitutable (VRIN).
- C1 agrees (firm-controlled). C2 **conflicts in tendency**: by lumping capabilities with assets and information it does not separate a disposition from its material basis or enablers. C3 conflicts in tendency: "organizational processes" are listed as resources alongside capabilities, not as their realization. C4–C6 silent. VRIN is an *adequacy/value* test (C6 adequacy), not a possession test — useful for the BCO's separation.
- Authority: seminal peer-reviewed.

**R3. Grant, R. M. (1991). "The resource-based theory of competitive advantage: implications for strategy formulation." *California Management Review* 33(3): 114–135. DOI 10.2307/41166664.** [from prior knowledge — verify]
- Licence/access: SAGE/UC Berkeley, paywalled.
- Definition (paraphrase): a capability is the capacity of a team of resources to perform some task or activity (approx. p. 119, to verify); resources are the source of capabilities, capabilities the main source of competitive advantage; capabilities rest on coordinated patterns among people and between people and other resources, i.e. routines.
- C1 agrees (team/organization-level capacity). C2 **partly agrees**: basis is a coordinated "team of resources" with people central, but non-human resources are inside the team rather than mere enablers. C3 agrees (capability exercised as routines/activities). C4–C6 silent.
- Authority: seminal peer-reviewed (practitioner-facing journal, but the canonical capability/resource distinction).

**R4. Amit, R. & Schoemaker, P. J. H. (1993). "Strategic assets and organizational rent." *Strategic Management Journal* 14(1): 33–46. DOI 10.1002/smj.4250140105.** [citation verified] — [search result (DOI, volume, pages)](https://papers.mewayz.com/paper/oa_W2102469678); open copy hosted on a course site (scanned, not text-extractable) — [Mahoney course PDF](https://josephmahoney.web.illinois.edu/BA545_Fall%202011/S11/Amit%20and%20Schoemaker%20(1993).pdf)
- Licence/access: Wiley/JSTOR, paywalled; an unofficial course-site scan exists.
- Definition (paraphrase, from prior knowledge — wording and p. 35 to verify): capabilities are a firm's capacity to deploy resources, usually in combination, using organizational processes, to effect a desired end; they are information-based, firm-specific, and developed over time through interactions among the firm's resources; resources are stocks of available factors owned or controlled by the firm.
- C1 agrees (firm's capacity). C2 **partly agrees**: capability is distinct from resources (good for separating disposition from enablers), but the basis is not named as member agents. C3 agrees strongly: capability *uses* organizational processes to effect an end — capability ≠ process. C4–C6 silent. The capacity/resources split is the closest RBV analogue to BFO disposition vs. its enabling conditions.
- Aggregator citation count ~8,285 (unverified aggregator figure) — [mewayz](https://papers.mewayz.com/paper/oa_W2102469678).
- Authority: seminal peer-reviewed.

### Inferences
- The RBV gives two incompatible taxonomies: capability-as-resource (Barney) and capability-as-capacity-to-deploy-resources (Grant; Amit & Schoemaker). Only the latter is compatible with C2/C3. The BCO should cite Grant and Amit & Schoemaker as support and record Barney as a known divergence.
- VRIN and "strategic asset" are value/adequacy notions (C6), not possession criteria; the RBV conflates "the firm has X" with "X yields rent", which the BCO's three-question separation explicitly avoids.

### Gaps
- Exact page and wording of the Barney, Grant and Amit & Schoemaker definitions were not confirmed from full text in this session (paywalls; the Amit & Schoemaker open copy is a JBIG2 scan the fetch tool could not read).

## Organizational capabilities and routines (Helfat & Peteraf 2003; Winter 2003; Dosi, Nelson & Winter 2000)

### Takeaway
Helfat & Peteraf's "ability of an organization to perform a coordinated set of tasks, utilizing organizational resources, for a particular end result" is the single best peer-reviewed anchor for C1 and C3, and their lifecycle model treats capabilities as things that are founded, develop and mature — compatible with a disposition whose basis changes. Winter's "high-level routine" definition is the main conflicting view: it makes the capability *a routine* (process-like) and locates the options with management.

### Cited Findings

**O1. Helfat, C. E. & Peteraf, M. A. (2003). "The dynamic resource-based view: capability lifecycles." *Strategic Management Journal* 24(10): 997–1010. DOI 10.1002/smj.332.** [citation verified] — [RePEc/IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/stratm/v24y2003i10p997-1010.html)
- Licence/access: Wiley, paywalled.
- Abstract (verified): introduces the capability lifecycle (founding, development, maturity, and branching into altered forms) to explain heterogeneity in organizational capabilities — [RePEc/IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/stratm/v24y2003i10p997-1010.html).
- Definition (paraphrase; wording and page — c. p. 999 — **not verified** this session, search did not surface the passage): an organizational capability is the ability of an organization to perform a coordinated set of tasks, using organizational resources, to achieve a particular end result. They distinguish *operational* capabilities from *dynamic* capabilities, and stress that capabilities originate with a team (a founding group of individuals) whose members and their coordination develop through practice.
- C1 **agrees** (bearer is the organization; "ability"). C2 **agrees in substance**: the lifecycle is driven by a team of people and their coordination; resources are *utilized*, i.e. enabling. C3 **agrees** (capability is exercised in coordinated tasks; the capability is not the tasks). C4 silent. C5 silent. C6 partly: the lifecycle describes possession over time; "end result" is purpose, not adequacy.
- Authority: seminal peer-reviewed.

**O2. Winter, S. G. (2003). "Understanding dynamic capabilities." *Strategic Management Journal* 24(10): 991–995. DOI 10.1002/smj.318.** (The brief named it "Understanding dynamic and ordinary capabilities"; the published title is "Understanding dynamic capabilities".) [citation verified] — [RePEc/IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/stratm/v24y2003i10p991-995.html); [Penn repository record](https://repository.upenn.edu/mgmt_papers/30)
- Licence/access: Wiley, paywalled; Penn ScholarlyCommons record exists (open-access status not confirmed).
- Abstract (verified, paraphrased): distinguishes "zero-level" (ordinary) capabilities that let a firm earn a living in the short run from dynamic capabilities that extend, modify or create ordinary ones; argues capability substance is patterned activity whose creation requires costly investment; change is also possible through "ad hoc problem solving" without dynamic capabilities — [RePEc/IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/stratm/v24y2003i10p991-995.html).
- Definition (paraphrase; from prior knowledge, p. 991, verify): an organizational capability is a high-level routine (or collection of routines) that, together with its input flows, gives an organization's management a set of decision options for producing significant outputs of a particular type.
- C1 agrees (organization). C2 partly: routines plus "implementing input flows" — enablers are inside the definition. C3 **conflicts**: capability *is* a (high-level) routine, i.e. process-like, rather than a disposition realized in processes. C4 tension: "decision options conferred on management" brings the authority/decision side into the definition. C5/C6 silent; the zero-level vs dynamic distinction is admissibility-relevant (two kinds of capability type).
- Authority: seminal peer-reviewed (short special-issue essay).

**O3. Dosi, G., Nelson, R. R. & Winter, S. G. (eds.) (2000). *The Nature and Dynamics of Organizational Capabilities*. Oxford University Press.** [from prior knowledge — verify ISBN/pages]
- Licence/access: OUP book, paywalled.
- Definition (paraphrase; introduction, verify): organizational capabilities are know-how that enables an organization to carry out characteristic activities; they are built from routines as building blocks, have a significant "size", and their exercise is recognizable as producing a characteristic output.
- C1 agrees. C2 partly (know-how embedded in people and their routines). C3 **mixed**: routines are the components of capabilities rather than their realization; this is closer to "capability = bundle of routines" than to disposition. C4–C6 silent.
- Authority: seminal edited scholarly volume.

### Inferences
- For a BFO reading, Helfat & Peteraf's "ability ... to perform a coordinated set of tasks" maps directly onto disposition (ability) + realization (performance of tasks) + bearer (organization). The evolutionary-economics line (Winter; Dosi, Nelson & Winter) is better read as an account of the *material basis* (routines held in people and their coordination) than as the capability itself — that reading keeps C3 intact while crediting their insight.
- Helfat & Peteraf's lifecycle (founding, development, maturity, branching) describes changes in possession and basis over time; the BCO can cite it for why possession (C6) is temporally indexed.

### Gaps
- The exact Helfat & Peteraf definitional sentence and page were not confirmed in this session (search returned only the abstract).
- Dosi, Nelson & Winter introduction wording and pages not checked.

## Dynamic capabilities (Teece, Pisano & Shuen 1997; Teece 2007; Eisenhardt & Martin 2000)

### Takeaway
Teece et al. define dynamic capability as the firm's *ability* (disposition-like); Eisenhardt & Martin define it as *processes/routines* (process-like). This is the clearest disposition-vs-process conflict in the literature, and Offerman et al. explicitly note the "ability versus capacity" dispute in a review they summarize. The ordinary/dynamic distinction (Winter; Teece) is a distinction between capability *types*, which falls under the BCO's admissibility question.

### Cited Findings

**D1. Teece, D. J., Pisano, G. & Shuen, A. (1997). "Dynamic capabilities and strategic management." *Strategic Management Journal* 18(7): 509–533.** [definition text verified via Offerman Table II; bibliographic data from prior knowledge — verify DOI 10.1002/(SICI)1097-0266(199708)18:7<509::AID-SMJ882>3.0.CO;2-Z]
- Licence/access: Wiley, paywalled.
- Definition (paraphrase): the firm's ability to integrate, build and reconfigure internal and external competences to address rapidly changing environments; Offerman et al. reproduce this as the dominant dynamic-capability definition (cited by six articles in their sample) — [Offerman et al. 2017, pp. 386–387, Table II](https://liacs.leidenuniv.nl/~plaata1/Offerman2017_SLR.pdf). Note: Offerman's Table II attributes it to "Teece et al. [23]", but their ref. [23] is Teece 2007, a citation slip.
- Short quote (Teece et al. 1997, via Offerman): "ability to integrate, build, and reconfigure internal and external competences".
- C1 agrees (firm's ability). C2 silent (competences, not basis). C3 agrees (the paper's "processes, positions, paths" frame treats managerial/organizational processes as where capabilities show up). C4–C6 silent.
- Authority: seminal peer-reviewed.

**D2. Teece, D. J. (2007). "Explicating dynamic capabilities: the nature and microfoundations of (sustainable) enterprise performance." *Strategic Management Journal* 28(13): 1319–1350. DOI 10.1002/smj.640.** [title verified as Offerman ref. [23] — [Offerman et al. 2017](https://liacs.leidenuniv.nl/~plaata1/Offerman2017_SLR.pdf); volume/pages/DOI from prior knowledge — verify]
- Definition (paraphrase): dynamic capabilities disaggregate into capacities to sense opportunities and threats, seize them, and reconfigure the enterprise's assets; their microfoundations are distinct skills, processes, organizational structures and decision rules.
- C1 agrees. C2 partly (microfoundations include structures and decision rules — the "arrangement"). C3 agrees (processes are microfoundations / where it shows, not the capability). C4 tension: managerial decision rules sit inside the microfoundations. C5–C6 silent.
- Authority: seminal peer-reviewed.

**D3. Eisenhardt, K. M. & Martin, J. A. (2000). "Dynamic capabilities: what are they?" *Strategic Management Journal* 21(10–11): 1105–1121.** [definition verified via Offerman Table II; bibliographic data from prior knowledge — verify]
- Licence/access: Wiley, paywalled.
- Definition (paraphrase): dynamic capabilities are organizational and strategic routines by which firms achieve new resource configurations as markets emerge, collide, split, evolve and die — [Offerman et al. 2017, Table II](https://liacs.leidenuniv.nl/~plaata1/Offerman2017_SLR.pdf). The paper treats them as specific identifiable processes (product development, alliancing, strategic decision making) with commonalities ("best practice") across firms.
- C1 agrees. C2 silent. C3 **conflicts**: the capability *is* the routine/process. C4–C6 silent. The best-practice claim is also relevant to C6: if capabilities are processes, adequacy (effectiveness) becomes a property of the process, which collapses possession into adequacy.
- Authority: seminal peer-reviewed.

**D4. Helfat et al. (2007) definition via Pult & Manwani.** Offerman et al. list "the capacity of an organisation to purposefully create, extend, or modify its resource base" as a dynamic-capability definition attributed to Pult & Manwani [36] — [Offerman et al. 2017, Table II](https://liacs.leidenuniv.nl/~plaata1/Offerman2017_SLR.pdf). From prior knowledge, this wording originates in Helfat, Finkelstein, Mitchell, Peteraf, Singh, Teece & Winter (2007), *Dynamic Capabilities: Understanding Strategic Change in Organizations* (Blackwell) — verify before citing. "Purposefully" and "capacity" support a disposition reading; it is the consensus definition Helfat and Teece converged on.

### Inferences
- The BCO can adopt the Teece / Helfat line (ability/capacity) and record Eisenhardt & Martin as the routine-as-capability dissent; under C3 their "routines" are the *realizations* (processes) or the *basis* (patterned arrangements of agents), not the capability.
- Ordinary vs dynamic capability is a distinction among capability types — an admissibility matter (C6): a dynamic capability is a disposition whose realizations change the organization's other dispositions or their bases. That is expressible in BFO without a new top-level category.

### Gaps
- Page numbers for Teece et al. (definition commonly cited at p. 516) and Eisenhardt & Martin (c. p. 1107) not verified in this session.

## Capability maturity (CMMI)

### Takeaway
CMMI rates *process* capability per process/practice area (capability levels 0–3) and *organizational* maturity across predefined sets of process areas (maturity levels 1–5). It is an assessment framework about processes, a different sense of "capability" from the BCO's; in BCO terms a CMMI rating is a description-layer ICE (C5) about adequacy (C6), and about process (realization) quality rather than the disposition.

### Cited Findings
- CMMI Institute: capability levels apply to performance and improvement in individual practice areas; maturity levels describe the organization as a whole — [CMMI Institute, Levels](https://cmmiinstitute.com/learning/appraisals/levels).
- Continuous representation rates each process area 0–3 (incomplete, initial, managed, defined); appraisal yields a capability-level profile — [CMMI Institute, Levels](https://cmmiinstitute.com/learning/appraisals/levels); maturity levels characterize improvement relative to a set of process areas, capability levels relative to a single process area — [wibas](https://wibas.com/cmmi/staged-representation-cmmi-acq) (secondary).
- Equivalent staging: capability level 2+ in the ML2 process areas is equivalent to maturity level 2 — [InformIT](https://www.informit.com/articles/article.aspx?p=28797) (secondary).
- Origin: Paulk, Curtis, Chrissis & Weber's Capability Maturity Model (SEI) is listed by Offerman et al. among "maturity model" frameworks, distinct from business capability frameworks — [Offerman et al. 2017, Table IV / ref. [61]](https://liacs.leidenuniv.nl/~plaata1/Offerman2017_SLR.pdf).
- Licence/access: CMMI model documents are proprietary (ISACA / CMMI Institute), paid. Authority: vendor/standards body (industry standard, not peer-reviewed).
- Bearing: C1 conflicts in sense (rated thing is a process area, not an organization's disposition; maturity level is about the organization but is defined by process achievement). C2 silent. C3 inverted (rates the realization). C4 silent. C5 **agrees**: a rating is an assessment artifact. C6 agrees: maturity is adequacy, not possession.

### Inferences
- The BCO should name CMMI as a homonym: "process capability" (CMMI) is a quality of a process type's instances, and a CMMI appraisal result is an ICE about adequacy of realizations. Using CMMI level as evidence of possessing a business capability conflates C6's questions.

### Gaps
- Official CMMI V2.x/V3.0 model definition of "capability level" was not read (proprietary).

## Business-architecture and enterprise-architecture practitioner literature

### Takeaway
Practitioner sources converge on capability as *what* the business does (or can do), stable and independent of *how* (process, organization unit, sourcing, automation). Homann (2006) is the hinge: one of his formulations ("a particular ability or capacity that a business may possess or exchange...") is disposition-like and is adopted almost verbatim by Offerman et al.'s synthesized definition, while his other formulation ("what the business does ... regardless of resources") is activity-like and explicitly excludes resources — the opposite of Wißotzki, who includes resources. Practitioner sources are **silent or conflicting on C2** (several treat capability as a bundle of people, process and technology).

### Cited Findings

**P1. Homann, U. (2006). "A Business-Oriented Foundation for Service Orientation." Microsoft MSDN Architecture Center, February 2006.** [existence verified via citing reference list](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145%2F2739011.2739021); original MSDN URL (msdn.microsoft.com/en-us/library/aa479368.aspx) now retired — the Microsoft Learn previous-versions path returned 404 in this session.
- Licence/access: was free online (Microsoft copyright); currently only via archives.
- Definitions (as reproduced by Offerman et al., Table II, PDF p. 5 = proceedings p. 387) — [Offerman et al. 2017](https://liacs.leidenuniv.nl/~plaata1/Offerman2017_SLR.pdf):
  - (a) capabilities are what the business does (e.g. pay employee, ship product) regardless of the resources used or how they are configured (in-sourced or outsourced, manual or automated).
  - (b) Short quote: "A particular ability or capacity that a business may possess or exchange" (to achieve a specific purpose or outcome).
- Offerman et al. note Homann's exclusion of resources contradicts Wißotzki's inclusion of them — [Offerman et al. 2017, p. 386](https://liacs.leidenuniv.nl/~plaata1/Offerman2017_SLR.pdf).

- Licence/access: IEEE paywalled; free author copy on Leiden/LIACS servers.
- Method: 720 Google Scholar results (Jan 2016), 103 abstracts naming a capability type, 20 analysed in depth — [p. 385](https://liacs.leidenuniv.nl/~plaata1/Offerman2017_SLR.pdf).
- Key findings: no dominant definition of business capability; Homann and Wißotzki contradict each other on whether resources are part of a capability; capability research descends from the RBV and from enterprise architecture, the latter describing capabilities as combinations of process, technology, economic goods and persons; only two business-capability frameworks found (TOGAF capability-based planning; Brits et al.) — [pp. 384, 386](https://liacs.leidenuniv.nl/~plaata1/Offerman2017_SLR.pdf).
- Synthesized definition (p. 387, based on Homann and Wißotzki): short quote, "A particular ability that a business may possess or exchange" (to achieve a specific corporate goal). Examples: electronic service delivery, Sarbanes–Oxley compliance, HR management, develop product or service, customer management. **Counter-examples include resources such as people or assets** — [p. 387](https://liacs.leidenuniv.nl/~plaata1/Offerman2017_SLR.pdf).
- C1 **agrees** (business possesses an ability). C2 **agrees**: people and assets are explicitly *not* capabilities — consistent with basis/enablers being distinct from the disposition. C3 agrees implicitly (ability, not activity). C4 silent. C5 silent-to-agrees (capability *maps* are distinguished as sets of capabilities for an industry/organization, p. 388). C6: "or exchange" raises sourcing; silent on adequacy.
- Authority: peer-reviewed (IEEE conference); the best single secondary source for the definitional landscape.

**P8. Other EA-stream sources visible through Offerman et al.** Enterprise architecture defines business capability as the combination of process, technology, economic goods and persons (Bredemeyer et al.; Ulrich 1987 introduced "organizational capability" from an HR-management angle) — [Offerman et al. 2017, p. 384, refs. [13]–[15]](https://liacs.leidenuniv.nl/~plaata1/Offerman2017_SLR.pdf). This "combination" view **conflicts with C2 and C3** (process and technology inside the capability).

### Inferences
- The BCO's cleanest practitioner anchors are Homann (b), Ulrich & Rosen's principles, and Offerman et al.'s synthesized definition with its counter-examples. Together they support C1, the enabler half of C2, and C3; Khosroshahi et al. and Ulrich & Rosen support C5.
- The practitioner phrase "possess or exchange" is the main hazard for C1: read literally it suggests a capability can be transferred. Under C6 this is better modelled as the *adequacy/sourcing* question (a sourcing statement is an ICE; an external provider bears its own capability).
- No practitioner source found states C2's positive claim (basis = member agents in arrangement). That claim has to be argued from Helfat & Peteraf (team origins), Grant (team of resources), and the routines literature, not cited from practitioners.

### Gaps
- Homann's original MSDN text could not be retrieved; definitions rely on Offerman et al.'s reproduction. A Wayback Machine copy of msdn.microsoft.com/en-us/library/aa479368.aspx should be checked.
- Ulrich & McWhorter (2010) definition, the BIZBOK Guide definition (Business Architecture Guild — which, from prior knowledge, uses essentially Homann's "particular ability or capacity ... possess or exchange" wording; verify), Wißotzki's *Capability Management Guide*, Keller's "Business Capability Mapping", and a Gartner glossary entry for "business capability" itself were not found or not retrieved. No reliable source for a Gartner analyst named "Scott" / "Burton" capability-modelling definition was found.

## Where the sources conflict

### Takeaway
Three fault lines run through the literature, and each maps onto a BCO commitment: (1) ability/capacity vs routine/process (C3); (2) resources and technology inside vs outside the capability (C2); (3) capability as possessed vs as measured by outcome/performance/maturity (C5/C6). The BCO's draft sides with the ability, resources-outside, and possessed-vs-assessed positions, each of which has peer-reviewed support, while each opposing position also has seminal authority, so the spec should record the divergence rather than claim consensus.

### Cited Findings
- **Ability vs routine.** Ability/capacity: Teece et al. 1997; Homann 2006 (b); Offerman et al. 2017 — [Offerman Table II](https://liacs.leidenuniv.nl/~plaata1/Offerman2017_SLR.pdf); Helfat & Peteraf 2003 (prior knowledge). Routine/process: Eisenhardt & Martin 2000 — [Offerman Table II](https://liacs.leidenuniv.nl/~plaata1/Offerman2017_SLR.pdf); Winter 2003, whose abstract makes patterned activity the substance of capability — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/stratm/v24y2003i10p991-995.html). Offerman et al. report a review that found a dispute over "ability versus capacity" in the dynamic-capability literature and concluded the approach is not yet a theory — [Offerman et al. 2017, p. 384](https://liacs.leidenuniv.nl/~plaata1/Offerman2017_SLR.pdf).
- **Resources/technology inside or outside.** Outside: Homann (regardless of resources, sourcing or automation); Beimborn et al.; Offerman et al. (people and assets are counter-examples); Ulrich & Rosen (automated capabilities are still business capabilities) — [Offerman](https://liacs.leidenuniv.nl/~plaata1/Offerman2017_SLR.pdf); [Cutter](https://www.cutter.com/article/business-capability-map-rosetta-stone-businessit-alignment-executive-summary-389961). Inside: Wißotzki (resources as an element), Bharadwaj (IT capability as ability to combine IT infrastructure and human IT skills), EA "combination of process, technology, economic goods and persons" — [Offerman pp. 384, 386](https://liacs.leidenuniv.nl/~plaata1/Offerman2017_SLR.pdf). Barney (capabilities are a kind of resource) is a third position (prior knowledge).
- **Possessed vs measured.** CMMI measures process capability/maturity by goal achievement — [CMMI Institute](https://cmmiinstitute.com/learning/appraisals/levels); Merriam-Webster's senses of capability (level vs availability), noted by Offerman et al. as a source of confusion — [Offerman p. 384](https://liacs.leidenuniv.nl/~plaata1/Offerman2017_SLR.pdf); capability-map use cases are assessments and decisions about capabilities — [Khosroshahi et al. 2018](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/handle/10125/50470).

### Inferences
- Offerman et al.'s observation that the dictionary senses split into *level* of a capability and *availability* of a capability lines up with the BCO's adequacy vs possession questions; it is a citable motivation for C6.
- The routine view can be reconciled with the BCO by treating routines as the arranged pattern in the material basis (C2) and their enactments as realizations (C3); the resources-inside view can be reconciled by treating technology as enabling conditions whose absence affects adequacy (availability), not possession.
- None of the surveyed management or practitioner sources addresses C4 (ability vs assignment/authorization/responsibility) directly; Ulrich & Rosen's separation of capability from business unit is the nearest support. C4 will need to rest on the BCO's own ontological argument (BFO disposition vs role/deontic ICE), not on this literature.

### Gaps
- No source in this set gives an explicit admissibility criterion (which types can be genuine capabilities). Offerman's examples/counter-examples are the closest.
- Page-level verification of the seminal journal definitions (Barney, Grant, Amit & Schoemaker, Helfat & Peteraf, Winter, Teece et al., Eisenhardt & Martin) remains to be done against publisher PDFs.
